I was sitting in the class. There’s a short petite figure in the distance. She’s standing and has a book in her hand. She’s reading it. She’s saying something while looking up occasionally.

There are roughly fifty other people in the room. I’m sitting on the fifth bench. The bench number determined our character in the class. First benchers were the best students. Seventh benchers were the worst. I was a fifth.

I’m near a window. Looking out. There’s a ground. Brown color soil. Not entirely. Some stones, incomplete and uncomfortable patches of grass. Someone ambitious planted them years ago and now no one bothers about the damn grass. I could see the auditorium on the second floor. Its windows. They have a very characteristic spoil pattern. No dirt in the middle. Pretty bad on the sides. Completely opaque, rusty brown in the corners. I knew what was inside. It was lecture time for the entire school. There was just absence of presence. No lights. No kids playing, no bullies. Stale air with dust particles trolling around each other, not travelling too far. It was as hollow as a dead planet which once had life.

I looked inside. Teacher was still attempting to teach. She was teaching for the first three benchers. Fourth people pretended to pay attention. Everyone else pretty much knew that this isn’t something they could take seriously. Who could take this seriously? First benchers were different. Who couldn’t take this seriously? Why did the first benchers take this seriously? There was something about the nature of these two people that made them hate each other.

I saw around. My crush was a sixth bencher. Full cheeks. Specs. Mesmerizing. So damn cute. I could spend the entire day looking at her. If only she doesn’t notice and look back at me. She notices me. She gave me a curious look. She knew I was looking. I quickly turned away. I couldn’t see her reaction after. She was in the first row, I was in fourth. Her bench partner smiled at her, as if they had an inside joke running about me.

I was now watching everyone in the middle. The girl who had no idea about anything. Who was she? Where was she? How did she come to be? All these deep philosophical questions were not for her, she couldn’t be bothered with it. Their existence or lack of it had no effect on her. She looked like she genuinely had no idea about anything. There were the drum brothers. They loved playing drums on the metal benches. The kind of music they played in Ganesh festivals. Creative people, wanted to just enjoy life with the energy they didn’t know what to do with. Forced to attend sermons on British Indian history.

The teacher calls me out. She looked like a frustrated woman stuck in the dead end job making pittance enough to keep her full. Scolding children was her coping mechanism. I stand up. She asks me questions. I answer. “Sit down and pay attention. Don’t look here and there.” I cringed at the rotten language. She said it in a way that burnt my brain in high frequency. “Yes miss,” I utter, pretending to be humiliated. She feels good about herself. Her dark droopy eyes light up. Leaves me alone. That’s what they want. Stroke their ego and they’re happy. First benchers did exactly that. I sit down. I continue with my visual adventures. She won’t bother me for two or three days now, four if I was lucky. Pretending to pay attention was something I was too lazy to do. I liked staying in my head.

The textbook had all the essential things. Lists of facts, dates, accounts on who performed which action which resulted in what. “Look at the history as a story,” they told us. But the history textbook wasn’t written like a story book. No one was telling us a story. Everyone knew that. Phoney teachers. They couldn’t be bothered to prepare for the lessons before they taught it. It was a damn propaganda. Pretending to sound so Bachelors in Education. We wanted to revolt, take over and replace the system which favoured us. That wasn’t in our control. So we refused to put up with this facade.

We hated the first benchers for accepting this. They used to put in efforts to understand this. We didn’t. We didn’t see the point in it. We had things we considered more important. They were wiser I’d say. Submitting to what’s expected of you and get the rewards. We were children. We didn’t need this. We needed inspiration. We needed a role model. Someone we could look up to. Someone who would awaken the spark in us. That would make us do things on our own. Everyone knew how hopeless these ideas were, but we stuck to it nevertheless.

Last benchers were the people who were more interested in other things. There were sportsmen, artists. There were smokers and alcoholics. There were academics who wanted to aim higher than the bureaucratic schools.

And then there were people like me and my crush. We lived life because we didn’t know what else to do. We existed because it was something given. We had no purpose, no passion. Drifted aimlessly and blissfully throughout our lives in school. Fooling around, making friends, fighting, making up. Seeking whatever pleasures of lives we could get our hands on. Being regular human children. Who’s to decide what’s regular? Because I wouldn’t trade my childhood with these first benchers or good or bad back benchers. I loved who I was, with problems and everything. The human I was.
